# The NASAmeter — Voice Calibration Tool

A diagnostic scale to calibrate voice for appropriate intensity. Credit: Agus di Carlo — "he's right."

The name: Posts that sound too grandiose, too much like you're announcing planetary importance, hit a NASA vibe. They get the astrophysics treatment when they should get the grocery store treatment.

## The Scale

| Level | Vibe | Appropriate For |
|-------|------|-----------------|
| **1–2** | Kitchen table conversation. You'd text this to a friend. | Personal stories, DMs, very casual community posts |
| **3–4** | Instagram. Real moment, real conviction, real stakes. | Primary target zone. Most content lives here. |
| **5–6** | LinkedIn thoughtful post. Good professional day. Maximum for Substack. | Long-form, serious frameworks, philosophical depth |
| **7–8** | TED talk audition. Sounding like you're on stage. | Rewrite. This is too loud. |
| **9–10** | Neil deGrasse Tyson explaining the universe with a whiteboard. You've invented a new field. | NEVER. This is the problem. |

## Diagnostic Checks

Ask these before hitting post:

### 1. Does the sentence need a dramatic pause after?
If yes → You're at NASA level. Bring it down.

**NASA version:** "The future of business is unfolding right now, and most founders are too busy optimizing to notice."

**Right version:** "Most founders are too busy optimizing to notice what's actually shifting."

### 2. Does it contain any of these words?
- Paradigm / shift
- Future / futures (unless specific)
- Access problem / access revolution
- Humanity / human transformation
- Evolution / revolution
- Transform / transformation (unless specific)
- Gravity
- Collapse
- Inevitable

If yes → You might be at NASA. Check the stakes.

### 3. What's the actual stakes?
Stakes should match the statement.

**NASA stakes (too big):** "This is reshaping how humanity thinks about work."
**Real stakes:** "This changes whether you can take a week off."

### 4. Would your friend text you this?
If no → It's probably too loud.

**Not friendly:** "The intersection of AI and identity is forcing a reckoning with the nature of work itself."

**Friendly:** "AI doesn't save time. It makes you face who you are when effort doesn't prove anything anymore."

### 5. Is a big word doing the emotional lifting that a specific detail should do instead?
Replace word with detail. Details do more work.

**NASA version:** "The paradigm shift that AI represents..."
**Right version:** "When a tool understands your business in plain English instead of requiring constant retraining..."

### 6. Did you just save the world in a Facebook post?
Did you position yourself as having solved something civilization-scale? On social media?

→ Rewrite. This is NASA.

## The 13-Point Voice Check

These 13 questions catch NASA-level problems before publishing.

### 1. Would a management consultant say this?
If yes → It's too generic. Replace with specific observation.

**Generic:** "Companies need better systems to scale effectively."
**Specific:** "Your VA learns your process every quarter because nothing's documented. That's not a people problem — it's a documentation problem."

### 2. Does it sound "nice" or "helpful"?
If yes → It's too soft. Make a statement instead.

**Nice:** "I think it's important to be really clear about your process."
**Sharp:** "You haven't documented your process. That's why everything breaks when you try to scale."

### 3. Does it make someone think "she's describing MY business"?
If yes → Keep it. This is good.

If no → It's too general. Get specific about a real problem or pattern.

### 4. Am I explaining or orienting?
Explanation: Step-by-step, holding hands, "here's how it works."
Orientation: "Here's the map. You know how to walk."

→ Always orient. Never explain.

**Explaining:** "Here's how to set up a system. First, you document your process. Then, you extract the decision patterns. Then, you connect it to your tools..."

**Orienting:** "Your process is already in your head. Document it. Now your tools can read it."

### 5. Am I selling or stating?
Selling: "Here's why you need this..."
Stating: "This is what's true."

→ Always state. Never sell.

**Selling:** "If you codify your intelligence, you'll finally be able to scale without burning out."

**Stating:** "Codified intelligence scales. Founder-dependency doesn't."

### 6. Do I have minimum 3 sentence fragments?
Count fragments. Minimum 3 per post.

**Fragments:** "Not because you're not smart. But because your intelligence isn't structured."

### 7. Do I have at least 2 em dashes?
Minimum 2 in anything over 200 words.

**Example:** "I connected a tool to my business this morning — and my eyes welled up."

### 8. Is there at least one "Not X, but Y" or negation pattern?
At least one per educational/diagnostic post.

**Pattern:** "This isn't a people problem. It's a pattern recognition problem."

### 9. Does the close feel like gravity or pitch?
Gravity: The weight of what's true.
Pitch: Trying to convince you.

→ Close with gravity. Let truth stand.

**Pitch close:** "Imagine what you could do if you finally had a system that worked for you!"

**Gravity close:** "The moment you codify your intelligence, three things become inevitable: your business scales, your clients feel understood before they hire you, and you finally have time."

### 10. Am I announcing enthusiasm where I should be demonstrating it?
Announcement: "I'm so excited to share..."
Demonstration: [The thing exists and it's good.]

→ Cut announcement. Show the thing.

**Announcement:** "I'm thrilled to introduce a completely new way to structure your business..."

**Demonstration:** "You've been building a job. Here's how to build a business instead."

### 11. Would someone say "she's excited" or "this is true"?
The goal is "this is true," not "she's excited."

If they'd say "she's excited" → The thing isn't strong enough. Rewrite.

### 12. Does my humor explain itself?
Good humor: Lands without explanation.
Bad humor: "This is funny because..."

→ Never explain funny. Either it lands or it doesn't.

**Bad (explains itself):** "I kept all my systems in Zapier chains held together by prayer — which is funny because it's actually kind of true."

**Good (lands without explaining):** "Zapier chains held together by prayer."

### 13. What question does this implicitly answer?
The answer should be "What's actually true?"

Not "Isn't this cool?" or "Don't you want this?" or "Shouldn't you try this?"

→ If the implicit question is anything other than "what's actually true?" → Rewrite.

## The Fix Pattern

When you catch a NASA problem:

1. **Identify the NASA language.** What word or phrase is too big?
2. **Ask: What's the actual stakes?** Not metaphorical stakes. Real stakes.
3. **Replace big word with specific moment.** "The paradigm shift in AI" → "When a tool understands your business in plain English."
4. **Shorten the sentence.** Let it land.
5. **Read it aloud.** Would you say it to a friend?

## NASA Examples (Things to Avoid)

### Example 1: Civilization Scale
**NASA:** "The digital revolution is forcing a complete reimagining of what it means to be human in the modern world."

**Right:** "AI shows you who you are when effort doesn't prove your worth."

### Example 2: Future Vague
**NASA:** "The future of work is being written right now, and it's more important than ever to understand the implications."

**Right:** "You can't take a week off. That's the problem we're solving."

### Example 3: Announcement of Importance
**NASA:** "Today I want to share something truly revolutionary about how businesses can transform..."

**Right:** (Skip announcement. Start with the thing.)

### Example 4: Paradigm Word Salad
**NASA:** "This represents a fundamental paradigm shift in how we conceptualize the relationship between human intelligence and technological architecture."

**Right:** "Your genius works for you. The tool reads it like a conversation partner instead of needing constant retraining."

### Example 5: Gravity Theft
**NASA:** "This will change everything about how you approach your business and your life forever."

**Right:** "Three things become inevitable: [specific list]."

## Remember the Rule

Specific stakes beat big words.

Real moments beat announcements.

Gravity beats pitch.

If it's true, you don't need to make it sound important.
